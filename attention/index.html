All About the KV Cache

Part 14 of How to Scale Your Model: The 2026 Update (Part 13: MoE | Part 15: Training in 2026)

Section 7 argued that for long sequences the KV cache, not the weights, sets the cost of generation. Here we work out the bytes, FLOPs and arithmetic intensity of the attention mechanisms that have replaced grouped-query attention since then: latent compression, sliding windows, sparse selection, linear-attention layers, and token compression. The cache per token shrank 30x in two years, and along the way attention stopped being purely memory-bound.

Worked Problems

Why Attention Is Back on the Critical Path

In Section 7 we found that a generate step takes at least as long as it takes to read every sequence’s KV cache and all the parameters from HBM. In Section 8 we saw that for LLaMA 3-70B the KV term dominates at any context beyond a few thousand tokens. Section 4 told us the MLP is where the FLOPs are, so why does attention need a whole section now? Because three things have changed since then.

Contexts got much longer. When Section 8 was written, LLaMA 3 had an 8k context (later extended to 128k). Kimi K3, DeepSeek-V4 and Qwen3.8 all serve a million tokens, and agentic workloads (a coding agent reading a repository, a research agent making a hundred tool calls) typically carry 100k to 500k tokens of context. At 128k tokens, LLaMA 3-70B’s 164kB-per-token cache is 21GB per sequence.Section 8 writes this as 160kB, but it's actually 2 * 8 * 128 * 80 = 163,840 bytes, which we round to 164kB here. Three of those come to 64GB, nearly filling an H100 and leaving almost no room for the weights!

Outputs got longer. A chat reply is a few hundred tokens, but a reasoning trace is 10k to 100k. Decode has always been the expensive half of serving per token, so if most of our tokens are now decoded rather than prefilled, that’s where we should expect the cost to go. In Section 8 we found that 8k-token prompts with 512-token outputs need three prefill servers per decode server. What if the outputs are 16k tokens instead? Writing $P$ and $G$ for the numbers of prefill and decode servers and reusing Section 8’s per-server throughputs, we get P / 0.91 = 32 * G / (0.019 * 16384), or about 11 decode servers per prefill server. Once outputs are this long, the decode roofline is the one we need to get right.

MLPs got sparse. In Section 13 we saw that attention is 30% of DeepSeek-V3’s active parameters, against 17% for LLaMA 3-70B. The sparse MLP each token sees is narrow, while attention is as big as it would be in a dense model; making the MLP sparse does nothing to attention’s FLOPs. In prefill and training, attention FLOPs overtake MLP FLOPs at about 19k tokens for DeepSeek-V3, where a dense model of the same width with $F = 4D$ would cross at 86k by the same formula. (Section 4’s $8D$ rule of thumb counts the FLOPs a bit differently, and we’ll come back to that below.)

So attention is expensive in bytes at decode, expensive in FLOPs at long context, and long context is far more common than it used to be. Let’s look at what we can do about it, and at what the recent models actually do.

Multi-Head Latent Attention

Multi-head latent attention (MLA) first appeared in DeepSeek-V2, and DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2 and K3, GLM-5 and Mistral Large 3 all use it. The idea is to cache a low-rank latent of the keys and values instead of the keys and values themselves.

In standard attention each layer projects the residual $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$ to $N$ query heads, $K$ key heads and $K$ value heads of dimension $H$, and caches the keys and values, $2KH$ elements per token per layer. MLA instead projects $x$ down to a single latent $c \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}$ with $d_c = 512$, plus a small decoupled key $k_R \in \mathbb{R}^{d_R}$ with $d_R = 64$ that carries the rotary position information.Rotary position embeddings (RoPE) have to be applied to the key before it's dotted with the query, but a rotated key can't be reconstructed from a shared latent with a per-head linear map. MLA's answer is to split each key into a "no position" part, reconstructed from the latent, and a small "rope" part that is computed and cached directly and shared across heads. This is why the configs have qk_nope_head_dim and qk_rope_head_dim. Each head then reconstructs its own key and value from the latent with per-head matrices $W_{UK}^{(h)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c \times H}$ and $W_{UV}^{(h)}$. The cache per token per layer is $d_c + d_R = 576$ elements. For DeepSeek-V3’s 61 layers that’s 35,136 elements, or 35kB in fp8, against 164kB for LLaMA 3-70B and 258kB for 405B.

Isn’t this just GQA with fewer heads? 576 elements is about what GQA with 2.25 KV heads of dimension 128 would cost. But those 2.25 heads would be shared by all 128 query heads, whereas MLA reconstructs 128 different keys and values from the latent. We get something as expressive as MHA and as cheap in bytes as MQA, and (as we’ll see) we pay for it in FLOPs.

Two things stand out. The output projection $W_O$ is 63% of attention’s parameters, because 128 heads of dimension 128 make a 16,384-wide concatenated output, more than twice the residual width. And the matrices that actually touch the latent are tiny. So MLA saves nothing in attention parameters (LLaMA 3-70B’s GQA layer has 151M, DeepSeek-V3’s MLA layer 187M). All the savings are in the cache, which is fine, since bytes read at decode were the problem in the first place.

The absorption trick changes the roofline

How do we use this cache at decode time? The naive way is to reconstruct every head’s keys and values from the latent for every past token. That costs 2 * 512 * 128 * (128 + 128) = 33.5M FLOPs per past token per layer (pushing the latent through $W_{UK}$ and $W_{UV}$ for all 128 heads), and worse, it materializes $S \cdot 128 \cdot 256$ elements of keys and values that the attention kernel then has to read. That would throw away the whole benefit. Instead we absorb the up-projections into the query and the output:

\[q_h^\top k_{h,s} = q_h^\top W_{UK}^{(h)\top} c_s = \tilde{q}_h^\top c_s \qquad \text{where} \quad \tilde{q}_h = W_{UK}^{(h)} q_h \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\]

We map each head’s query once into the 512-dimensional latent space, compute scores against the shared latent $c_s$ (plus the 64-dimensional rope term), take the softmax-weighted sum of the latents to get a 512-dimensional output per head, and apply $W_{UV}$ afterwards. The attention kernel never sees a per-head key or value. It sees one 576-wide “key” and one 512-wide “value” per token, shared by all 128 heads, which is essentially MQA with an unusually wide head.

Now let’s count FLOPs. Per layer, per decoded token, per context token, absorbed attention does

\[2 \cdot N \cdot (d_c + d_R) + 2 \cdot N \cdot d_c = 2 \cdot 128 \cdot (576 + 512) = 278{,}528 \text{ FLOPs}\]

and reads $576$ bytes (fp8). That’s an arithmetic intensity of 484 FLOPs/byte with an fp8 cache, or 242 with bf16. Compare GQA: LLaMA 3-70B does $2 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 32{,}768$ FLOPs per context token and reads $2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 = 2048$ bytes, an intensity of 16 (int8) or 8 (bf16). That’s just the group size $G = N/K$, as Question 4 of Section 4 says.

Now let’s compare with the hardware. For the GPUs, the accelerator intensity (bf16 FLOPs/s over HBM bandwidth) is 296 for an H800 or H100, 206 for an H200, 281 for a B200 and 312 for a GB200. For the TPUs it’s 311 for TPU7x, 240 for v5e and 164 for v5p.These come from Section 12, Section 2 and the hardware table in the 2026 outline. GQA attention at intensity 8 is hopelessly memory-bound on all of them, which is why Section 7 could treat decode attention as always bandwidth-bound. MLA attention at 484 FLOPs/byte with an fp8 cache is above the roofline on every one of them. With a bf16 cache (242) it’s above the H200 and the two older TPUs, but 14 to 22% below the H800, B200, GB200 and TPU7x. So with MLA and an fp8 cache, decode attention is compute-bound at long context.

Put another way, DeepSeek-V3 does 8.5x the attention FLOPs of LLaMA 3-70B per context token but reads 3.6x fewer bytes. Since bytes were what bound us, that’s a good trade.

Note [precision]: the 484 figure assumes an fp8 cache paired with bf16 score and value matmuls.If the attention matmuls themselves ran in fp8, the accelerator intensities would double (591 for H800/H100, 412 for H200, 562 for B200, 625 for GB200, 622 for TPU7x), and 484 would be memory-bound on the H800, B200, GB200 and TPU7x and compute-bound only on the H200. The rest of this section assumes bf16 matmuls on an fp8 cache. We assume an fp8 cache for V3 throughout, as Section 16 does.DeepSeek's V3 write-up says the attention core runs in bf16 but not how the cache is stored. fp8 seems safe: DeepSeek's FlashMLA kernels support it, and DeepSeek-V4 says it uses an fp8 cache with bf16 RoPE dimensions.

Why does this matter in practice? You can see one consequence in DeepSeek’s serving layout (Section 16): attention runs data-parallel across all 144 decode GPUs, each GPU handling attention for its own sequences, rather than tensor-parallel across heads. Tensor parallelism over heads would make every shard read the full shared latent for every sequence (the latent isn’t sharded by head), so each GPU would do the same FLOPs as under data parallelism but read $Y$ times the bytes. That divides the arithmetic intensity by $Y$ and pushes attention back toward memory-bound. Data parallelism keeps each sequence’s latent on one GPU, so the intensity stays at 484 and the attention FLOPs available grow with the number of GPUs.

It also means we can treat the number of heads as a hardware knob. The intensity of absorbed MLA is proportional to $N$, so the head count can be tuned to the FLOPs-to-bandwidth ratio of whatever chip we serve on. The GLM-5 report says as much: “the number of attention heads in DeepSeek-V3 is selected according to the roofline of H800”, which “is inappropriate for other hardware”. GLM-5 raised the head dimension from 192 to 256 and cut the head count by a third relative to its own baseline, landing at 64 heads. Kimi K2 also went with 64 heads, and its report gives the trade: 128 heads would have cost 83% more inference FLOPs for a 0.5 to 1.2% gain in loss.

Let’s put in some numbers. For DeepSeek-V3 at 128k context, one decoded token’s attention costs 61 * 278528 * 131072 = 2.2e12 FLOPs and reads 61 * 131072 * 576 = 4.6GB. At batch 64 on a B200 that’s 64 * 2.2e12 / 2.25e15 = 63ms of FLOPs against 64 * 4.6e9 / 8e12 = 37ms of bytes: 63ms per step for attention alone, before any MoE work. This is exactly the problem DeepSeek Sparse Attention was built to fix, as we’ll see shortly.

Takeaway: MLA caches a shared 576-element latent per token per layer instead of $2KH$ keys and values, which cuts DeepSeek-V3’s cache to 35kB per token. With the absorption trick, the decode kernel behaves like MQA with a 576-wide head shared by all 128 query heads, for an arithmetic intensity of 240 to 480 FLOPs/byte. With an fp8 cache, attention is compute-bound at long context on every current GPU, so we scale it by adding GPUs in data parallel.

Sliding Windows and Local-Global Interleaving

The oldest trick is simply not to attend to everything. A sliding window layer attends only to the previous $w$ tokens, so its cache holds at most $w$ entries however long the context gets. Models interleave a few global layers that see everything with many local ones:

Model Pattern Window Global layers
Gemma 3 27B 5 local : 1 global 1,024 10 of 62
Gemma 4 26B-A4B 5 local : 1 global 1,024 5 of 30
gpt-oss-120b alternating 128 18 of 36
Llama 4 Maverick 3 chunked : 1 global 8,192 (chunk) 12 of 48

The cache per token as a function of context is then

\[\text{bytes per token}(S) = 2 K H \cdot \text{bytes} \cdot \left[ L_\text{global} + L_\text{local} \cdot \min\left(1, \frac{w}{S}\right) \right]\]

Once $S \gg w$ only the global layers count, and we save a factor of $L / L_\text{global}$: 6x for Gemma, 4x for Llama 4, 2x for gpt-oss. Gemma 4 adds two more tricks in its global layers: only 2 KV heads (against 8 in the local layers), and keys reused as values (attention_k_eq_v in the config). Its global cache is $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 512 = 1024$ bytes per layer per token, 5kB per token in total. That’s 8x smaller than Gemma 3 27B at long context, in a model of about the same size.

What goes wrong with windows? There are two problems, and models need a fix for each. The first is the attention sink. When the earliest tokens fall out of the window, attention loses the place it used to park excess probability mass, and quality collapses. gpt-oss adds a learned per-head bias to the softmax denominator, a slot that attends to nothing, so the excess mass has somewhere harmless to go, and DeepSeek-V4 has “learnable attention-sink logits” for the same reason. The second problem is positions: a global layer with ordinary RoPE has to handle relative distances far beyond anything it saw in training. Llama 4’s global layers use no positional encoding at all (NoPE),Llama 4 calls this interleaving of RoPE and NoPE layers "iRoPE". Gemma 4’s use a low-frequency variant, and Muse Glimmer copies Llama 4’s pattern. The idea is that the local layers carry the positional signal and the global layers only have to retrieve. This is what makes 1M- and 10M-token context claims possible without retraining the position embedding.

What about FLOPs? A local layer costs $2 N H \cdot 2 w$ per token, a constant. Only the global layers have the quadratic term, so the attention-versus-MLP crossover of Section 4 moves out by $L / L_\text{global}$. For Gemma 3 27B it’s over 500k tokens.

Takeaway: interleaving local and global layers divides the growing part of the cache by $L / L_\text{global}$ and leaves a fixed $L_\text{local} \cdot w \cdot 2KH$ bytes per sequence. With a 1024-token window and one global layer in six, plus K=V and 2 heads in the global layers, Gemma 4 gets down to 5kB per token. Sinks and position-free global layers keep it stable.

Sparse Attention: Index, Then Attend

Sliding windows choose what to attend to by position. What if we chose by content instead? That’s sparse attention: a cheap indexer scores every past token, we select the top $k_a$, and full attention runs over only those. The version we’ll work through is DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA), which DeepSeek shipped in V3.2. It grew out of a research design called Native Sparse Attention, and GLM-5 and MiniMax M3 adopted variants of it within months.

DSA sits on top of MLA, so everything from the previous section carries over. Its lightning indexer has $H_I = 64$ small heads of dimension $d_I = 128$. It computes one 128-dimensional indexer key per token, shared across the 64 heads, and scores past token $s$ for query $t$ as

\[I_{t,s} = \sum_{j=1}^{64} w_{t,j} \cdot \text{ReLU}(q^I_{t,j} \cdot k^I_s)\]

in fp8, with a ReLU rather than a softmax so that nothing has to be normalized across $S$. We then select the top $k_a = 2048$ latents by score and run the absorbed MLA attention over just those 2048. Per layer, per decoded token, this costs

  FLOPs bytes (fp8)
Indexer, per context token $2 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 = 16{,}384$ 128
Main attention, fixed $278{,}528 \cdot 2048 = 570\text{M}$ $2048 \cdot 576 = 1.2\text{MB}$

Per context token, the indexer is 17x cheaper in FLOPs than full MLA attention and reads 4.5x fewer bytes, and the expensive attention no longer grows with context at all. At 128k context, DeepSeek-V3.2 reads 1.1GB per decoded token where V3 reads 4.6GB, 4x fewer bytes. It does 0.17 TFLOPs where V3 does 2.2, 13x fewer FLOPs. On a B200 at batch 64 the attention step drops from 63ms (compute-bound) to about 9ms (now memory-bound on the indexer keys). You can see this in DeepSeek’s published cost curves, which show decode cost per token nearly flat in context length.Less scientifically, DeepSeek halved its API price the day V3.2-Exp (the experimental release before V3.2) shipped.

Where does the remaining cost go? At 1M context, the indexer accounts for 99% of the bytes and 97% of the FLOPs of V3.2’s attention. There are two ways to shrink it, and both show up in shipped models. We can compute the index in fewer layers: GLM-5.2 computes it in only 21 of its 78 layers and reuses the selected positions in the other 57 (indexer_types and index_topk_freq in its config), which cuts its reads at 128k to 0.44GB. Or we can make each indexer key smaller: DeepSeek-V4 compresses the indexer keys 4:1 and stores them in fp4, as we’ll see below.

How expensive is DSA to train? DeepSeek made V3.2 from V3.1 with continued pretraining in two stages. First it trained only the indexer for 2.1B tokens, with everything else frozen, using a KL loss against the head-summed softmax so the indexer learns to match the real attention distribution. Then it turned sparse attention on and trained for 944B tokens. That’s only 6% of the original pretraining budget for a 4x cut in decode bytes.

Note [TPUs]: top-$k$ selection over $S$ scores followed by a gather of 2048 latents at data-dependent addresses is natural on a GPU (a radix-select kernel and a scattered read). On a TPU it’s the same kind of dynamic-shape, gather-heavy operation that makes MoE routing awkward, and it needs a custom kernel. None of this rules sparse attention out on a TPU, but it helps explain why it showed up on GPUs first.

Takeaway: sparse attention replaces the 576-byte-per-context-token attention read with a 128-byte indexer read, plus a fixed attention over 2048 selected entries. For DeepSeek-V3.2 that’s 4x fewer bytes and 13x fewer FLOPs than full MLA at 128k. The indexer becomes the cost at very long context, so newer models share it across layers or compress its keys.

Linear Attention Hybrids: State Instead of Cache

The most radical option is to get rid of the growing cache in most layers altogether. A linear attention or state-space layer keeps a fixed-size matrix state per head, updated once per token, instead of a list of past keys and values. Qwen3-Next, Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.8 use Gated DeltaNet; Kimi Linear and Kimi K3 use a variant with a per-channel decay called Kimi Delta Attention (KDA); and Nemotron 3 uses Mamba-2. All of them keep a minority of ordinary attention layers, since pure linear attention can’t do exact retrieval.

The delta-rule recurrence, per head with key dimension $d_k$ and value dimension $d_v$, is

\[\Sigma_t = \Sigma_{t-1} \left( \gamma_t I - \beta_t k_t k_t^\top \right) + \beta_t v_t k_t^\top, \qquad o_t = \Sigma_t q_t\]

where $\Sigma_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v \times d_k}$ is the state, $\gamma_t$ is a learned decay and $\beta_t$ is a learned write strength.We write $\Sigma$ because $S$ is the context length throughout this book, and $\gamma$ rather than the literature's $\alpha$ because the rest of the book keeps $\alpha$ for arithmetic intensity. You can think of it as an online least-squares update to an associative memory. For our purposes, what matters is that the state has $d_k d_v$ elements per head, is read and written once per decoded token, and doesn’t depend on $S$ at all.

Model Linear : full Linear heads x $d_k$ x $d_v$ State per layer Layers State per sequence
Qwen3.5-397B 3 : 1 64 x 128 x 128 4.2MB (fp32) 45 189MB
Qwen3.8-2.4T 3 : 1 128 x 128 x 128 8.4MB (fp32) 69 579MB
Kimi K3 3 : 1 96 x 128 x 128 3.1MB (bf16) 69 217MB
Nemotron 3 Super ~5 : 1 128 x 64 x 128 (Mamba-2) 4.2MB (fp32) 40 168MB

Note that these states are large. Qwen3.8 reads and writes 1.16GB of state per sequence per decode step, at any context length. Its 23 full-attention layers (4 KV heads of 256) read 47kB per context token, so state traffic equals cache traffic at 1.16e9 / 47e3 = 24,600 tokens. Below that the fixed state dominates the hybrid’s step, and above it the growing cache does. The comparison that matters, though, is against an all-attention model of the same shape (92 GQA layers, 188kB per token). The hybrid reads less than that once 1.16e9 / (188e3 - 47e3) = 8,200 tokens are in context. At 1M tokens, Qwen3.8 reads 50GB per decoded token, where the all-GQA model would read 92 * 2048 * 1048576 = 198GB.

For Kimi K3, the same two calculations say the state stops dominating at about 31k tokens, and the hybrid beats an all-MLA 93-layer stack above about 11k.Storing the state in bf16 halves these numbers. Qwen ships with an fp32 state (mamba_ssm_dtype: float32), presumably because the recurrence accumulates error, while Kimi doesn't say and we assume bf16. This is a real design knob: the state is written every step, so its precision is more delicate than that of a KV cache, which is written once.

So a hybrid only pays off at long context. The state is also a per-sequence cost that batching doesn’t amortize (each sequence has its own), so it behaves like a fixed-length KV cache, not like weights. The FLOPs, on the other hand, are trivial. Updating a $128 \times 128$ state per head costs about $8 d_k d_v$ FLOPs, or 8 * 128 * 128 * 128 = 16.8M per layer for Qwen3.8, against 2 * 8.4MB = 16.8MB of fp32 state read and written. That’s an intensity of about 1 FLOP per byte (2 with a bf16 state), so the linear layers are memory-bound like GQA and unlike MLA.

What about training? There the recurrence is computed chunk-wise. Within a chunk of 64 tokens everything is a matmul, and states are passed between chunks. FLOPs are $O(S d_k d_v)$ per head with no quadratic term. The same linear cost is what lets Qwen3-Next claim over 10x the inference throughput of Qwen3-32B beyond 32k tokens of context. Context parallelism becomes a sequential pass of a few megabytes of state between GPUs rather than an AllGather of keys and values (Kimi K3’s report describes a dedicated scheme for this).

Will every model end up a hybrid? It isn’t clear yet, and MiniMax shows how unsettled this is: MiniMax M1 shipped a 7:1 linear hybrid in mid-2025, MiniMax M2 went back to full attention four months later, and MiniMax M3 moved to block-sparse attention instead. Qwen and Kimi use hybrids, DeepSeek uses sparsity plus compression, and Google uses windows. If you look back at the table at the start of the chapter, though, the 2025 and 2026 flagships all store between about 5 and 50kB per token, within a factor of ten of each other (DeepSeek-V4.1 is the outlier, below 1kB). So while the labs have chosen different mechanisms, they seem to have settled on roughly the same number of bytes per token.

Takeaway: linear-attention layers replace a growing cache with a fixed state of $d_k d_v$ elements per head, read and written every step. For 2026 models that’s hundreds of megabytes per sequence, so hybrids read more than an all-attention model of the same shape below roughly 8 to 11k tokens of context, and win by 3 to 4x at 1M. The linear layers are memory-bound, their state is a per-sequence cost that batching doesn’t amortize, and they’re cheap to train at long context.

Token Compression: DeepSeek-V4

Finally, DeepSeek-V4 combines most of the above and adds one new idea: compress the KV cache along the sequence before storing it. Its attention has three components, all sharing one cache format:

  • A single shared KV entry per token of 512 dimensions, the last 64 of which carry RoPE, used as both key and value.In the config: num_key_value_heads: 1, head_dim: 512, qk_rope_head_dim: 64. It’s stored as 448 fp8 plus 64 bf16 elements, 576 bytes per entry, so it matches MLA’s 576 in bytes through mixed precision rather than in element count. You can think of this as MQA with a single, very wide KV head. V4-Pro has 128 query heads, each 512 wide and projected from a 1536-dimensional query latent as in MLA, and each head does 2 * (512 + 512) = 2048 FLOPs per selected entry.
  • Compressed Sparse Attention (CSA), in half the layers. A small learned module compresses every 4 consecutive tokens into one entry, an indexer (64 heads of 128, in fp4) scores the compressed entries, and attention runs over the top 1024 (Pro) or 512 (Flash) of them plus a 128-token uncompressed sliding window.
  • Heavily Compressed Attention (HCA), in the other half. Every 128 tokens become one entry, and attention runs over all of them with no sparsity.

The layers alternate CSA, HCA, CSA, HCA. The stored cache per token is roughly $(576 + 64) / 4$ bytes for a CSA layer and $576 / 128$ for an HCA layer, so over 30 CSA and 31 HCA layers it comes to about 4.9kB per token. DeepSeek quotes this as 10% of V3.2 (our estimate is 11.5%). The bytes read per decoded token at context $S$ are

\[30 \cdot \left[ \frac{S}{4} \cdot 64 + 1024 \cdot 576 + 128 \cdot 576 \right] + 31 \cdot \left[ \frac{S}{128} \cdot 576 + 128 \cdot 576 \right]\]

which works out to 0.1GB at 128k and 0.67GB at 1M, against 8.3GB for V3.2 and 37GB for V3 at 1M. The FLOPs fall similarly, to about 0.2 TFLOPs per token at 1M, 5x below V3.2 and 80x below V3. At 1M context, DeepSeek itself says V4-Pro needs 27% of V3.2’s per-token FLOPs for the whole model and 10% of its cache. It also says the cache is about 2% of what a GQA model with 8 KV heads of dimension 128 would need in bf16.That's about 4% of the same cache stored in fp8, the convention we use elsewhere in this section.

What happened to the indexer problem? Compression shrinks the indexer as well. Indexer keys are stored per compressed entry (one per 4 tokens), in fp4 (64 bytes), so the indexer reads 16 bytes per original token per layer instead of 128. The term that was 99% of V3.2’s bytes is now eight times smaller.

V4.1-Flash goes further in two ways. It shares the compressed KV entries and indexer keys across layers, so only 4 of its 40 layers produce a cache at all (kv_source_layer_ids: [2, 8, 14, 20]), and it stores that cache in fp4 (the 128-token window stays fp8). Together these get it to a published 890 bytes per token, 184x smaller than LLaMA 3-70B’s cache, with roughly 2x of the saving coming from fp4 and 2x from the sharing.V4.1 is a causal encoder-decoder: the first 20 layers compress 2:1 and the last 20 run 1:1, with no HCA layers. Its 196B of Engram memory tables (hashed n-gram embeddings fetched from host memory) sit outside the 552B backbone.

Takeaway: DeepSeek-V4 stores one 576-byte entry per 4 tokens (sparse-selected) or per 128 tokens (dense), plus a 128-token raw window, for about 5kB per token stored and 0.7GB read per token at 1M context. Compressing along the sequence multiplies with every other trick in this section, including shrinking the indexer that limited V3.2.

Rooflines: Decoding With a Modern Cache

Now let’s return to Section 7’s step-time formula, which needs one amendment. With MLA-class attention, attention’s FLOPs term is no longer negligible, so

\[\begin{aligned} T_\text{step} \geq \max\Big( &\frac{B \cdot \text{bytes read}(S) + \text{weight bytes per GPU}}{W_\text{hbm}}, \\ &\frac{B \cdot \text{attention FLOPs}(S) + 2 B \cdot \text{active params}}{C} \Big) \end{aligned}\]

where $B$ is the per-GPU batch in sequences, $\text{bytes read}(S)$ comes from the table above, and the weight bytes per GPU are the model’s weights divided by however many GPUs they’re spread over. (The MoE FLOPs term uses all the active parameters, since each of the GPU’s own tokens visits $k$ experts somewhere.) Let’s see what this gives for a few models at 128k context on a GB200 NVL72 rack, which has 72 GPUs with 186GB each in one NVLink domain (see Section 12). We’ll spread every weight, attention included, over 64 of the GPUs, since a power of two lets the experts divide evenly:

Model Weights per GPU Cache per sequence (stored) Sequences per GPU
LLaMA 3 405B, int8 6.3GB 33.8GB 5
DeepSeek-V3, fp8 10.5GB 4.6GB 38
DeepSeek-V3.2, fp8 10.7GB 5.6GB (with indexer keys) 31
Qwen3.8-2.4T, fp8 37.5GB 6.8GB 22
Kimi K3, fp4 experts (as shipped) 24.4GB 2.0GB 80

With everything sharded, a dense GQA model from 2024 (the LLaMA 3 row) fits only five 128k-token sequences per GPU, against 38 for an MLA model and 80 for a hybrid. One caveat: those rows shard the attention weights too. Under the data-parallel attention layout from the MLA section, V3’s attention, shared-expert, dense and embedding weights (17GB) are replicated on every GPU, and Section 16 gets 27GB of weights per GPU for V3 in total. That leaves room for (186 - 27) / 4.6 = 34 sequences for V3 and 28 for V3.2. Kimi K3’s 56GB of non-expert weights are the largest replicated block, and it fits about 54.

What about the hardware DeepSeek actually serves on? The same arithmetic is tighter there. Under 144-way expert parallelism (EP, Section 13), each H800 holds 22GB of weights (Section 16) and has about 58GB free, which is 58 / 4.6 = 12 full-length sequences at 128k. The same layout on an H200 fits 25. On TPUs the numbers are about the same: a 64-chip TPU7x cube, with 192GB per chip, comes within 4% of every row in the table above, fitting one more sequence per chip for the MLA and GQA models and three more for Kimi K3.

That’s the memory side. For the time side, let’s compare DeepSeek-V3 and V3.2 at batch 32 per GPU on a GB200 (8e12 bytes/s, 2.5e15 FLOPs/s), with the weights spread over the rack as above:

  bytes per step FLOPs per step $T_\text{step}$ tokens/s per GPU
V3 (full MLA) 32 x 4.6 + 10.5 = 158GB 32 x 2.2e12 (attn) + 32 x 7.4e10 (MoE) = 7.3e13 max(20ms, 29ms) 1,100
V3.2 (DSA) 32 x 1.1 + 10.7 = 46GB 32 x 1.7e11 + 32 x 7.4e10 = 7.8e12 max(5.8ms, 3.1ms) 5,500

Full MLA is compute-bound on attention at this context, which no model in Section 8 ever was. DSA shrinks both terms and leaves us memory-bound at about 5x the throughput. This table accounts for a good part of why long-context API prices fell so far between 2025 and 2026.

What does this do to disaggregation? In Section 7 we moved the KV cache from prefill to decode servers over the network and called the cost “typically acceptable”. A 128k-token DeepSeek-V3 cache is 4.6GB. At the 50GB/s one-way that each GPU’s 400Gb/s InfiniBand NIC provides (Section 12), that’s 92ms, a few decode steps. A LLaMA 3-405B cache at the same length is 34GB, or 0.7 seconds! Small caches are what make it possible to disaggregate prefill and decode at long context at all.

Takeaway: the Section 7 decode roofline needs an attention-FLOPs term. For MLA models at long context it’s the binding term, and sparse attention removes it. Cache size sets how many sequences fit per GPU and, through the KV transfer, whether prefill-decode disaggregation is feasible at long context. At 128k on a GB200 with everything sharded, that’s 5 sequences for LLaMA 3-405B, 38 for DeepSeek-V3 and 80 for Kimi K3. V3 drops to 34 with attention replicated, and to 12 on the H800s it’s actually served on.

Rooflines: Attention FLOPs at Long Context

Section 4 showed that dot-product attention FLOPs exceed all other matmul FLOPs (QKVO projections included) once $T > 8D$, assuming non-causal MHA and $F = 4D$. Neither assumption holds anymore, so we’ll redo the comparison for causal attention (which halves the score FLOPs) and compare it against the MLP alone. Under Section 4’s original assumptions this comparison gives $S^\ast = 12D$ rather than $8D$, which is why LLaMA 3-70B appears at 86k in the table below rather than Section 4’s 65k, even though the model hasn’t changed.

Let’s write $d_{qk}$ and $d_v$ for the query-key and value head dimensions (both equal to $H$ in Section 4, 192 and 128 for DeepSeek’s MLA), and as in Section 13 let $k$ be the active experts and $E_s$ the shared ones. Per token per layer, with causal attention, the score and value matmuls cost $N (d_{qk} + d_v) S$ FLOPs in the layers that have full attention (the factor of 2 for multiply-add and the factor of $1/2$ for causality cancel), and the MLP costs $6 (k + E_s) D F$. Setting them equal over the whole model gives

\[S^\ast = \frac{6 (k + E_s) D F}{N (d_{qk} + d_v)} \cdot \frac{L}{L_\text{full}}\]
Model $N$ $d_{qk} + d_v$ MLP per token per layer $L_\text{full} / L$ $S^\ast$
LLaMA 3 70B 64 256 6 x 8192 x 28672 1 86k
LLaMA 3 405B 128 256 6 x 16384 x 53248 1 160k
DeepSeek-V3 128 320 6 x 9 x 7168 x 2048 1 19k
Kimi K2 64 320 6 x 9 x 7168 x 2048 1 39k

Qwen3.8-2.4T 64 512 6 x 11 x 8192 x 2048 23/92 135k
Kimi K3 96 320 2 x (16 x 3 x 3584 x 3072 + 2 x 3 x 7168 x 3072 + 2 x 7168 x 3584) 24/93 180k
Gemma 3 27B 32 256 6 x 5376 x 21504 10/62 525k

Look at the DeepSeek-V3 row first. Going from dense LLaMA 3 to a fine-grained MoE with 128 heads pulls the crossover in from 86k to 19k tokens, so with a sparse MLP, attention is the expensive part of a 32k-token training sequence. How do the 2026 models deal with this? Each one changes a different term in the formula. Kimi K2 halves the number of heads, which halves $N$ and gains 2x. Qwen3.8 and Kimi K3 make three quarters of their layers linear, which gains 4x through $L / L_\text{full}$, and Gemma’s sliding windows in five layers of six gain 6x the same way. Sparse attention works differently. With DSA the quadratic term is the indexer, at $H_I d_I S = 8192 S$ per token per layer, which gives $S^* \approx 97\text{k}$ for V3.2, and the main attention becomes a constant.

When even that isn’t enough, or when a single sequence’s activations don’t fit, we reach for context parallelism (see Section 5’s note on it). For attention layers it’s cheap. Ring attention passes each GPU’s KV shard around the ring, S * 576 bytes per layer for MLA, a rounding error next to the $S^2$ FLOPs it enables. For linear layers it’s a sequential hand-off of the state between chunks. Most 2026 reports mention some form of it. Kimi K3 trained at 64k tokens and extended to 1M in its cooldown phase (the low-learning-rate tail of pretraining), while DeepSeek-V4 trained at 64k and reached 1M with the YaRN rescaling described in the next section.

Takeaway: the attention-versus-MLP crossover is $S^\ast = 6(k + E_s) D F L / (N (d_{qk} + d_v) L_\text{full})$. Fine-grained MoEs with many heads pulled it down to 19k tokens (DeepSeek-V3), and hybrids, windows and sparsity pushed it back past 100k. This, as much as the cache, is what makes it affordable to train 2026 models at long context.

Positions and Context Extension

A natural question is how a model trained at 4k to 64k tokens can serve a million.

Almost every model still uses RoPE and extends it with YaRN, which rescales the rotary frequencies so that positions beyond the training length map into the trained range. The config tells us the recipe:

Model Trained at Extended to Method
DeepSeek-V3 4k, then 32k, then 128k 128k YaRN factor 40 from 4k, two 1000-step phases
gpt-oss-120b 4k 128k YaRN factor 32
DeepSeek-V4 4k, 16k, 64k, 1M 1M YaRN factor 16 from 64k
Qwen3.8 262k native 1M YaRN factor 4
Kimi K3 8k, 64k; cooldown 256k to 1M 1M none: MLA layers have no positional encoding
Llama 4 Scout   10M (claimed) NoPE global layers; chunked local layers

Can we shrink the part that needs extending? There are two ways to do this. The first is partial RoPE, which rotates only a slice of each head (64 of 192 query dimensions in MLA, 64 of 256 in Qwen3.5 and 3.8, and 64 of 512 in DeepSeek-V4). The second is NoPE, which removes positions from the global layers entirely (Llama 4, Muse Glimmer, Kimi K3), on the idea that the local or recurrent layers carry position and the global layers only need content. Kimi K3 takes this furthest. Its linear layers encode position through their decay and its MLA layers have none, so, as its report puts it, it “extrapolates directly to 1M-token contexts without any positional-encoding modification.”

Why does this matter for systems? It matters mostly for the training schedule, since only a few percent of tokens are ever spent at the final length. DeepSeek-V3, for example, spent 2,000 of roughly 240,000 training steps on context extension, and V3.2’s sparse-attention conversion was 6% of pretraining. When budgeting a training run, we can treat the long-context phase as a short one tacked on at the end, and many of the architectural choices in this section are made precisely so that we can get away with that.

What Should You Take Away from this Section?

  • The stored cache per token, in fp8, depends on the mechanism. GQA-8 with 128-dim heads is $2048 L$ bytes (164kB for LLaMA 3-70B’s 80 layers) and MLA is $576 L$ (35kB for 61 layers). Interleaved windows divide the growing part by $L / L_\text{global}$, hybrids keep it only in the full-attention layers plus a fixed state of $d_k d_v$ per head, and DeepSeek-V4’s compression gets to about 5kB (0.9kB for V4.1’s fp4 cache).

  • The bytes read per decoded token can be far below the bytes stored. Sparse attention reads 128 bytes of indexer key per context token plus a fixed 2048 entries, which cuts DeepSeek-V3.2’s reads 4x at 128k relative to V3 and keeps them nearly flat in context.

  • MLA’s absorbed decode has an arithmetic intensity of 240 to 480 FLOPs/byte, so with an fp8 cache attention is compute-bound at long context, which it never was with GQA. Sparse attention cuts those FLOPs 13x. Data-parallel attention across the expert-parallel (EP) group is the layout that follows.

  • Linear-attention states are hundreds of megabytes per sequence and are read and written every step, so hybrids only beat full attention past about 8 to 11k tokens, and win 3 to 4x at 1M.

  • The attention-versus-MLP FLOPs crossover is $6 (k + E_s) D F L / (N (d_{qk} + d_v) L_\text{full})$: 19k tokens for DeepSeek-V3 and over 100k for the 2026 hybrids.

  • We can treat long context as a short training phase (made possible by YaRN, partial RoPE and position-free global layers), since only a few percent of a pretraining run is spent at the final length.

Worked Problems

Question 1 [cache sizes]: Let’s compare two cache designs. GLM-5.2 has 78 layers of MLA (latent 512 + 64) with a DSA indexer (128-dimensional keys) computed in 21 layers and reused in the rest. Kimi K3 has 24 MLA layers and 69 KDA layers with 96 heads of $128 \times 128$ state in bf16. For each, what is the stored cache per token, the fixed per-sequence state, and the bytes read per decoded token at 128k context? Assume one byte per element, and count one read and one write of recurrent state.

Click here for the answer, once you’ve thought about it!

GLM-5.2: The stored cache per token is 78 * 576 + 21 * 128 = 44.9kB + 2.7kB = 47.6kB, with no fixed state. At 128k, the 21 indexers read 21 * 131072 * 128 = 352MB and all 78 layers read 2048 selected latents, 78 * 2048 * 576 = 92MB, for 444MB in total. Storing a 128k sequence costs 47.6e3 * 131072 = 6.2GB, of which only 7% is touched per step.

Kimi K3: The stored cache per token is 24 * 576 = 13.8kB, since only the MLA layers grow. The KDA state is 69 * 96 * 128 * 128 * 2 bytes = 217MB per sequence. At 128k we read 24 * 131072 * 576 = 1.81GB of latents plus 2 * 217MB = 434MB of state traffic, or 2.25GB. At 8k the same calculation gives 113MB + 434MB = 547MB, of which 80% is state, so at short context the hybrid’s fixed cost dominates.

Question 2 [Gemma 3 versus Gemma 4]: Let’s compare the two Gemma generations. Gemma 3 27B has 62 layers with 16 KV heads of 128: 10 global layers and 52 local layers of window 1024. Gemma 4 26B-A4B has 30 layers: 5 global layers with 2 KV heads of 512 that reuse keys as values, and 25 local layers with 8 KV heads of 256 and window 1024. How many bytes does each read per decoded token at 8k and 256k context, and why is the ratio what it is?

Click here for the answer.

Gemma 3’s global layers read 10 * 2 * 16 * 128 = 41kB per context token, and its local layers hold a fixed 52 * 1024 * 4096 = 218MB. At 8k that’s 41e3 * 8192 + 218e6 = 554MB, and at 256k it’s 10.7GB + 0.2GB = 10.9GB.

Gemma 4’s global layers read 5 * 1 * 2 * 512 = 5.1kB per context token (the factor of 1 rather than 2 is the K=V trick), and the local layers hold 25 * 1024 * 4096 = 105MB. At 8k that’s 42MB + 105MB = 147MB, and at 256k it’s 1.34GB + 0.1GB = 1.44GB.

So the long-context ratio is 7.6x. Per global layer, Gemma 4 stores 1024 bytes per token against Gemma 3’s 4096 (2 heads instead of 16 is 8x fewer, 512 dims instead of 128 is 4x more, and K=V is 2x fewer), and it has half as many global layers. At 8k the fixed local caches are a large share of the total for both models, so the ratio is only 3.8x.

Question 3 [MLA intensity]: Kimi K2 and GLM-5 use MLA with 64 query heads rather than DeepSeek-V3’s 128. What is the arithmetic intensity of their absorbed decode attention with an fp8 cache and with a bf16 cache? On which of H800/H100, H200, B200, GB200, TPU v5e, v5p and TPU7x is it compute-bound?

Click here for the answer.

Per context token per layer, we do 2 * 64 * (576 + 512) = 139,264 FLOPs and read 576 bytes (fp8) or 1152 (bf16), so the intensity is 242 (fp8) or 121 (bf16). The accelerator intensities in bf16 FLOPs are 296 (H800/H100), 206 (H200), 281 (B200), 312 (GB200), 240 (v5e), 164 (v5p) and 311 (TPU7x). With an fp8 cache, 64-head MLA is compute-bound on the H200 and v5p, right at the line on v5e, and memory-bound on the H800 and H100, B200, GB200 and TPU7x, 14 to 22% below their rooflines. With a bf16 cache it’s memory-bound everywhere. Halving the heads relative to DeepSeek-V3 puts these models back on the memory-bound side of the line, which is probably where we want to be, given how expensive the FLOPs turned out to be for V3. Since MLA’s intensity is proportional to $N$, the head count really is a design knob.

Question 4 [where DSA’s cost goes]: Say we run DeepSeek-V3.2 at 1M context. How many bytes and FLOPs does it cost per decoded token, and what fraction of each is the indexer? How much does GLM-5.2’s scheme of indexing in 21 of 78 layers save at that length? What does DeepSeek-V4 do to the indexer term?

Click here for the answer.

Work per layer at $S = 2^{20}$. The indexer reads 1048576 * 128 = 134MB and the main attention reads 2048 * 576 = 1.2MB, while the indexer does 16384 * 1048576 = 17.2G FLOPs and the main attention 0.57G. Over 61 layers that’s 8.3GB and 1.08 TFLOPs per token, 99% and 97% of it indexer. GLM-5.2’s approach, scaled to V3.2’s 61 layers (indexing in 16 of them), would read 16 * 134MB + 61 * 1.2MB = 2.2GB, 3.7x less. DeepSeek-V4 stores one 64-byte fp4 indexer key per 4 tokens, so its indexer reads 16 bytes per original token per CSA layer, 30 * 1048576 * 16 = 0.5GB at 1M across its 30 CSA layers. That’s 16x less than V3.2’s indexer, and the main attention over 1024 compressed entries plus a 128-token window adds another 20MB.

Question 5 [hybrid break-even]: Qwen3.8 has 23 full-attention layers with 4 KV heads of 256 and 69 Gated DeltaNet layers with 128 heads of $128 \times 128$ state. At what context length does the per-step state traffic (one read, one write) equal the full-attention cache traffic if the state is fp32? What if it’s bf16? How does this compare with a DeepSeek-V3 sequence?

Click here for the answer.

The state per layer is 128 * 128 * 128 * 4 = 8.4MB in fp32, so over 69 layers it’s 579MB, and one read plus one write is 1.16GB per step. The full layers read 23 * 2 * 4 * 256 = 47kB per context token, so the two are equal at 1.16e9 / 47.1e3 = 24,600 tokens. In bf16 the state traffic halves and the break-even drops to 12,300 tokens. A DeepSeek-V3 sequence reads 61 * 576 = 35kB per context token, so 1.16GB is what V3 reads at 33k tokens. In other words, a Qwen3.8 sequence costs as much state traffic per step as a 33k-token DeepSeek-V3 sequence, whether it’s 1k or 1M tokens long!

Question 6 [V4 versus its predecessors]: Using the formula from the token-compression section, how many bytes does DeepSeek-V4-Pro read per decoded token at 128k and at 1M? How does that compare with V3.2 (DSA) and V3 (full MLA) at 1M?

Click here for the answer.

At 128k, the CSA layers read 30 * (32768 * 64 + 1024 * 576 + 128 * 576) = 30 * (2.1MB + 0.59MB + 0.07MB) = 83MB and the HCA layers read 31 * (1024 * 576 + 128 * 576) = 20MB, for 0.10GB in total. At 1M the CSA layers read 30 * (16.8MB + 0.66MB) = 524MB and the HCA layers 31 * (8192 + 128) * 576 = 149MB, for 0.67GB. V3.2 at 1M reads 8.3GB (12x more), and V3 reads 61 * 1048576 * 576 = 37GB (55x more). The sliding-window branch, present in all 61 layers, is only 61 * 128 * 576 = 4.5MB of fixed state per sequence, which is negligible.

Question 7 [attention crossover]: Derive the crossover $S^\ast$ in the table above for Kimi K3: 18 active experts of width 3072 on a 3584-wide MoE bottleneck (the projected-down residual of Section 13, unrelated to the MLA latent), 96 MLA heads with $d_{qk} + d_v = 320$, and 24 full-attention layers out of 93. What would $S^\ast$ be if K3 used MLA in every layer?

Click here for the answer.

Start with the MLP FLOPs per token per layer. The 16 routed experts operate on the 3584-wide bottleneck (16 * 3 * 3584 * 3072 = 528M parameters), the two shared experts run at full width (2 * 3 * 7168 * 3072 = 132M), and the bottleneck’s down- and up-projections add 2 * 7168 * 3584 = 51M, for 712M active MLP parameters or 1.42G FLOPs. Attention costs 96 * 320 = 30,720 FLOPs per context token per full layer. So S* = 1.42e9 * 93 / (30720 * 24) = 180k tokens. With MLA in all 93 layers, $L / L_\text{full} = 1$ and S* = 46k. The 3:1 hybrid buys 3.9x in the training crossover, on top of the decode savings we saw earlier.
