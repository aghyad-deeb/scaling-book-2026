How to Think About Mixture of Experts

Part 13 of How to Scale Your Model: The 2026 Update (2026 Update: Outline | Part 14: KV Cache)

Nearly every frontier open-weight model released since this book was written is a Mixture of Experts, and most are far sparser than the MoEs of 2024. This section extends the Transformer math of Section 4 and the parallelism rooflines of Sections 5, 7 and 12 to these models: how to count their parameters and FLOPs, why sparse models need enormous batches, what expert parallelism costs on GPUs and TPUs, and why the router is as much a systems component as a modeling one.

What Changed Since 2024?

So you want to run one of the newest open-weight models. It’s almost certainly a Mixture of Experts (MoE): nearly every open model above about 30B parameters is one now. Section 4 gives MoEs about two paragraphs, which made sense in early 2025, when the reference open model was the dense LLaMA 3. The MoEs of 2026 are also sparse in a different way from the ones Section 4 describes. They have hundreds of small experts, a handful of which are active per token. They hold 32 to 64 times as many experts as they activate (an $E/k$ sparsity of 32 to 64), which makes the total parameter count roughly 20 to 33 times the active count. This changes our rooflines quite a lot, as we’ll see. As usual, we care less about why MoEs are a good modeling idea than about what they cost to run.

A few things stand out:

  • Total parameters grew about 20x, but active parameters only about 2.5x. From Mixtral 8x22B to Kimi K3, the total went from 141B to 2.8T while the active count went from 39B to 104B. Nearly all the new parameters are ones that any given token never touches.
  • Sparsity went from 4 to between 32 and 64. The ratio $E/k$, which Section 4 calls the sparsity, was 4 for Mixtral. Everything released since DeepSeek-V3 is at 16 or more, and the 2026 models sit between 32 and 64.
  • Experts got narrow. Mixtral’s experts were 16,384 wide, as wide as a dense MLP. DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM and Qwen use hundreds of experts between 1,024 and 3,072 wide. This “fine-grained” design is now the default, and Llama 4 is the exception (128 experts of width 8,192, one active per token).
  • Dense models stop at about 30B. Qwen3.6-27B and Gemma 4 31B are the largest recent dense open models from their labs. Mistral is the exception, with dense 123B (Devstral 2) and 128B (Medium 3.5) open weights. Nearly everything above that size is sparse.

Why does this matter? Every roofline in this book compares something proportional to FLOPs, which scales with active parameters, against something proportional to bytes, which for weights scales with total parameters. In a dense model the two are the same, and the rules of thumb we’ve been carrying around, like “FSDP is compute-bound above 2,500 tokens per GPU across nodes” (Section 12) or “decode is compute-bound above batch 300”, assume the two are the same. An MoE with sparsity 32 pulls them apart by a factor of 32. The book already sketches the fix: Question 8 of Section 4 and Question 5 of Section 7 multiply the decode roofline by $E/k$, and Section 12 does the same for data parallelism and derives a GPU expert-parallel roofline. In this section we’ll work those results out properly. We’ll start with fine-grained experts, then work out expert parallelism (EP), first on the GPUs these models were actually trained and served on and then on TPU tori, and finish with EP combined with FSDP. On the GPU side that means the H800 with its cut-down NVLink, the GB200 NVL72 rack, and the InfiniBand between them.

MoE Transformer Math

Let’s fix notation. An MoE layer replaces the single MLP block of Section 4 with $E$ routed experts, each a gated MLP with its own three matrices $W_\text{in1}[D, F]$, $W_\text{in2}[D, F]$ and $W_\text{out}[F, D]$, plus (usually) a small number $E_s$ of shared experts that every token passes through. A router $W_r[D, E]$ scores each token against each expert, and the top $k$ scores decide which experts the token visits. The outputs of those $k$ experts are combined with weights derived from the router scores and added to the shared experts’ output.

Note [sigmoid routing and shared experts]: The original MoE papers softmax the router scores across experts and pick the top $k$. DeepSeek-V3 and most 2026 models instead apply a sigmoid to each score independently, pick the top $k$, and renormalize the selected weights to sum to one. DeepSeek-V4 uses the square root of a softplus. These choices matter to us mainly through load balancing, which we’ll discuss below. The shared expert is a plain dense MLP that every token goes through, so from a systems standpoint it’s a dense layer that happens to sit next to the MoE, and we shard it like one.

Counting parameters

Per MoE layer, the parameter count is

\[P_\text{MoE layer} = \underbrace{(E + E_s) \cdot 3 D F}_{\text{experts}} + \underbrace{E D}_{\text{router}}\]

and the number of parameters a single token touches is

\[P_\text{MoE layer, active} = (k + E_s) \cdot 3 D F + E D\]

The router is negligible. For DeepSeek-V3 it’s 256 * 7168 = 1.8M parameters per layer, against 11.3B for the experts. The whole model is

\[P_\text{total} = L_\text{MoE} (E + E_s) 3DF + L_\text{dense} \cdot 3 D F_\text{dense} + L \cdot P_\text{attn} + 2 D V\]

where several models keep the first one to three layers dense (first_k_dense_replace in DeepSeek-style configs), since routing works poorly on barely-embedded tokens.DeepSeek-V4 went the other way and made those first layers MoE as well, but with hash routing: the expert is chosen by a hash of the token id rather than by a learned router. That removes the router from those layers entirely, and for systems purposes it means the dispatch pattern is known before the layer runs. The attention here is Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA), which Section 14 takes apart. For now we’ll take its parameter count as given.

Let’s do the accounting for DeepSeek-V3, the same way Section 6 did it for LLaMA 3-70B. From the config: $D=7168$, $L=61$ (3 dense, 58 MoE), $E=256$, $E_s=1$, $k=8$, $F=2048$, $F_\text{dense}=18432$, $V=129280$.

That matches too. But notice how big attention has become. In the dense accounting of Section 6, the MLP was 80% of LLaMA 3-70B’s parameters and attention only 17%. Here attention is 30% of the active parameters. The MLP a token actually passes through is 9 experts of width 2,048, an effective width of 18,432 against a 7,168-wide residual stream, so it no longer dwarfs attention. We can’t wave attention away the way we mostly could for dense models, which is why all of Section 14 is about it.

Takeaway: an MoE has $L (E + E_s) 3DF$ parameters to within a few percent, and $L (k + E_s) 3DF$ active parameters plus attention. The ratio of the two is not $E/k$, since shared experts and attention count in both: DeepSeek-V3 has $E/k = 32$ but a total-to-active ratio of 18. When you see a “sparsity” quoted, check which one is meant.

Counting FLOPs

How many FLOPs does an MoE cost? The $6 \cdot \text{params} \cdot \text{tokens}$ rule from Section 4 still holds, as long as we plug in active parameters. Routing adds $2BDE$ FLOPs per layer, which is negligible, and each token does its $k$ expert matmuls and nothing else. So DeepSeek-V3 costs 6 * 37e9 = 2.2e11 FLOPs per token to train, about one eleventh of LLaMA 3 405B’s 6 * 405e9 = 2.4e12, while holding 65% more parameters.

This is the whole point of the design, and it shows in the pretraining budgets of the models people actually use:

Even DeepSeek-V4-Pro, trained on 33T tokens, used about a quarter of LLaMA 3 405B’s compute.Not every lab publishes token counts anymore. At 40% of an H100's bf16 peak, that's 2.1e6 * 3600 * 9.9e14 * 0.4 = 3e24 FLOPs, or about 100T tokens at 5.1B active parameters. That seems high, so the utilization or the token count is probably lower than this guess.

The ratio of training tokens to active parameters is also huge by Chinchilla standards: 400 tokens per active parameter for DeepSeek-V3 and 1,600 for Qwen3-235B, against the 20 tokens per parameter that’s compute-optimal for a dense model if training is the only cost you count. Why overtrain so much? Inference cost scales with active parameters, so it pays to spend extra training tokens keeping the active count small, and the large total parameter count makes up the quality we’d otherwise lose.

Takeaway: training an MoE costs $6 \cdot \text{active params} \cdot \text{tokens}$ FLOPs. The frontier open models of 2025 and 2026 were trained for 3e24 to 1e25 FLOPs, less than LLaMA 3 405B, while holding 2 to 7 times as many parameters.

Fine-grained experts

Why do nearly all recent models use hundreds of experts of width 1,024 to 3,072 rather than eight of width 16,384? The modeling argument is combinatorial. Choosing 8 of 256 experts gives the model $\binom{256}{8} \approx 4 \times 10^{14}$ possible MLPs per token, against $\binom{8}{2} = 28$ for Mixtral, at the same active FLOPs. DeepSeekMoE’s ablations found that splitting each expert four ways and activating four times as many beat the coarse design at equal active FLOPs, and its 16B model matched LLaMA 2 7B with about 40% of the compute.

What does this cost on the systems side? Every matmul gets smaller. A dense LLaMA 3-70B MLP matmul is [B, 8192] x [8192, 28672], while a DeepSeek-V3 expert matmul is [t_e, 7168] x [7168, 2048], where $t_e$ is the number of tokens that landed on that particular expert. This has two consequences:

  1. The matmuls are small. A [t_e, 7168] x [7168, 2048] matmul with a few hundred rows is at most a few dozen Tensor Core tiles of work on an H800, nowhere near enough to keep 132 streaming multiprocessors busy on its own. DeepSeek and others wrote grouped-matmul kernels (DeepGEMM, and the ragged kernels in every MoE framework) that run all of a GPU’s local experts in one launch, so the GPU sees one big ragged matmul instead of many tiny ones. Sharding an expert eight ways with tensor parallelism would leave each shard 256 wide, a single tile, so any padding or imbalance would waste a large fraction of the unit. The same is true on a TPU, where $F = 2048$ is eight 256-wide MXU tiles and an eight-way shard is a single tile. So we mostly won’t shard within experts. We’ll shard across them.

  2. The batch dimension is $t_e$ rather than $B$. On average $t_e = Bk/E$. It has to be large enough to fill the matmul unit’s tile, and for the matmul to be compute-bound rather than bound on loading weights, it also has to exceed the accelerator’s arithmetic intensity, which is 296 in bf16 on an H800.A Hopper Tensor Core instruction takes 64 rows, Blackwell 128, and a TPU systolic array 128 or 256. The bf16 intensity is 296 on an H800 or H100, 206 on an H200, 281 on a B200 and 311 on TPU7x (Section 12, Section 2). With $E/k = 32$, the batch has to exceed about 32 * 296 = 9,500 tokens per layer-step on an H800 before the experts stop being memory-bound. That’s a lot of tokens! For inference it’s the single most important consequence of sparsity, and we’ll keep coming back to it.

Takeaway: fine-grained experts turn one big matmul into $E$ small ones, each with an effective batch of $Bk/E$. Any efficiency argument in this book that relied on a large batch now has to be made with $Bk/E$ in place of $B$.

Routing Is a Systems Decision

In a dense model we (and the compiler) know the shape of every matmul before the program runs. In an MoE we don’t. The router decides at runtime how many tokens each expert gets, which sets the shape of every expert matmul and the pattern of every AllToAll. So although routing sounds like a modeling question, three things about the router matter a lot for systems.

Load balance. If expert 17 receives twice the average number of tokens, the GPU holding expert 17 has twice the work, and every other GPU waits for it. The efficiency of an expert-parallel layer is roughly $\text{mean load} / \text{max load}$ across GPUs. The classic fixes were an auxiliary loss that pushes the router toward uniform usage, and a capacity factor, which gives each expert a fixed buffer of $c \cdot Bk/E$ slots and drops any tokens that overflow it. Both have costs: dropping tokens hurts quality, and the auxiliary loss competes with the language-modeling loss. So DeepSeek-V3 and the 2025 models after it got rid of both.

DeepSeek-V3 adds a per-expert bias to the router score, used only for the top-$k$ selection, and after every step nudges the bias up for underloaded experts and down for overloaded ones. Kimi K3 goes a step further and sets each bias from a quantile of the router scores so that every expert receives exactly its target load, computing the quantile from a histogram that is AllReduced across the cluster. None of the models in our table drop tokens. Instead the expert matmuls are ragged (in JAX, jax.lax.ragged_dot), and balance is enforced by bookkeeping during training.

The other way to balance load is to duplicate hot experts. DeepSeek does this at inference time: its serving system holds 32 redundant copies of routed experts per 32-GPU prefill unit and rebalances which experts get copies based on the load it observes. Kimi K3’s training system does the same thing during training. It plans redundant experts per micro-batch so that every rank receives exactly $Bk/Z$ token copies, where $Z$ is the number of GPUs the experts are spread over (the expert-parallel degree, defined below). A nice side effect is that every matmul shape is static, so the host never has to synchronize with the device to learn how big the next matmul is.Static shapes matter a lot here. In a conventional MoE implementation the host has to wait for the router to finish before it knows the size of each expert's input and can launch the expert kernels, which stalls the pipeline at every layer. With guaranteed balance the shapes are known in advance. If you've used XLA, this is the same reason dynamic shapes are painful on TPU.

Locality. The router also decides how far each token’s activations have to travel. DeepSeek-V3 limits each token’s 8 experts to at most 4 of the 8 nodes that hold the experts (n_group = 8, topk_group = 4 in the config). It first picks the 4 best nodes by summed affinity and then the 8 best experts within them. That halves the cross-node copies of each token from 8 to 4, and it’s a change to the routing function made purely for the network’s sake. DeepSeek-V4 removed the constraint. The same report describes a fused dispatch, expert-matmul and combine kernel that hides most of the AllToAll behind compute, which is presumably what made the constraint unnecessary.

Latent routing. Kimi K3 and NVIDIA’s Nemotron 3 Super project each token down before dispatch, $z = W_\downarrow x \in \mathbb{R}^{\ell}$, with $\ell = D/2$ for K3 (3,584 of 7,168) and $\ell = D/4$ for Nemotron. The routed experts operate on $z$, and a $W_\uparrow$ maps the combined result back to $D$. The shared experts still see the full $x$. This halves (or quarters) the bytes that cross the network in both AllToAlls and shrinks each routed expert’s matrices to $[\ell, F]$. It doesn’t change the ratio of expert FLOPs to dispatch bytes, since both scale with $\ell$, but it cuts the absolute cost, which is what sets the step time when we’re communication-bound.

Takeaway: the router sets the shape of every expert matmul and the pattern of every AllToAll. Modern models keep it balanced with per-expert biases instead of dropped tokens, sometimes limit where a token’s experts can live to save network bandwidth, and sometimes shrink what gets sent. You can think of much of this as being done for the hardware’s sake.

What Sparsity Does to Memory

The obvious cost of a 2.8T-parameter model is that we have to store 2.8TB of it, even in fp8. Let’s see what that does.

What matters is how much of this fits inside one NVLink domain or ICI cube. An 8-GPU H800 node has 640GB of HBM and holds none of these models. An 8-GPU H200 node has 1.13TB, enough for DeepSeek-V3 with room for cache and for Kimi K2 with none. An 8-GPU B200 node has 1.44TB, which holds V4-Pro’s 0.87TB fp4 checkpoint with room for cache but not K3’s 1.56TB. The idealized 0.5 bytes per parameter would give 1.42TB and 0.83TB, but the shipped checkpoints are 1.56TB and 0.87TB, and we use the shipped sizes throughout. A GB200 NVL72 rack (72 GPUs, 13.4TB) holds every model in the table with terabytes to spare, and so does a 64-chip TPU7x cube (12.3TB). On 2024 hardware, though, these models don’t fit in a node, so we have to shard the weights across nodes. As Section 7 showed, the only sharding we can afford at decode time is model parallelism, which moves activations rather than weights. Expert parallelism is a form of model parallelism, so it’s the natural default for serving an MoE, and on H800s it has to cross InfiniBand.

Four-bit weights help a lot here. Kimi K3 and DeepSeek-V4 both ship their expert weights in a 4-bit microscaling format (MXFP4), produced with quantization-aware training, which halves the weight footprint relative to fp8 and doubles the batch that fits. Section 15 covers how that’s done.

Training. During training we have to store weights, gradients and optimizer state. Section 5 used 10 bytes per parameter (bf16 weights plus two fp32 Adam moments), which is 6.7TB for DeepSeek-V3 and 28TB for Kimi K3.K3 actually trained with Muon, which Section 15 shows brings this down to 6 bytes per parameter and 17TB. We use Section 5's 10 bytes here so we're comparing like with like. That sounds like a lot, but it gets sharded over the whole cluster: K3’s 28TB across 2,048 GPUs is only 14GB per GPU, and across a 9,216-chip TPU7x pod it’s 3GB per chip. The Kimi K2 report gives us one measured number to check against. Its bf16 weights and fp32 gradient buffers for 1.04T parameters come to about 6TB. Spread over its 256-GPU model-parallel group that’s 24GB per GPU, or about 30GB once the sharded optimizer state is added. So per-GPU weight memory isn’t the problem in training. Three other things are:

  • We can’t train on a small cluster. The “how few chips can I train on” exercise from Section 6 gives 28e12 / 80e9 = 350 H800s for Kimi K3 before any activations, or 28e12 / 192e9 = 146 TPU7x chips. With that section’s two bf16 checkpoints per layer, a 60M-token batch adds 2 * 7168 * 60e6 * 2 * 93 = 160TB of activations, so the real floor is about 188e12 / 80e9 = 2,350 H800s or 980 TPU7x chips. Compare LLaMA 3-70B, whose weights and optimizer state needed 0.7e12 / 96e9 = 8 v5p chips (Section 6 found 117 once activation checkpoints were included). Activations still dominate for an MoE, but by 6x rather than 15x, since sparsity grew the weights by $E/k$ without growing the activations.
  • The optimizer state is 32 to 64 times larger relative to the compute per step than for a dense model. FSDP’s AllGather and ReduceScatter traffic is proportional to weight bytes, while the FLOPs that have to hide it are proportional to active parameters. Below we’ll see this inflate the FSDP roofline by $E/k$.
  • Checkpoints are enormous. A Kimi K3 checkpoint with optimizer state is 17 to 28TB depending on the optimizer, and a run writes one every few hours.

The activations, by contrast, look just like those of a dense model whose MLP width is $(k + E_s) F$. There’s nothing new there, except that the ragged expert inputs are awkward to checkpoint (Kimi K3’s report has a section on a custom activation manager for exactly this reason).

Takeaway: an MoE’s weights are $E/k$ times larger than those of a dense model with the same per-token cost. At inference this forces us to shard the model across many GPUs just to hold it (across nodes, on anything older than an NVL72 rack), which is a big part of why 4-bit weights matter so much. In training it inflates every roofline that compares weight bytes to FLOPs by $E/k$, even though per-GPU memory at cluster scale stays manageable.

The Batch-Size Rooflines

Now let’s redo the two batch-size rooflines that dense-model intuition gets most wrong.

Decode. In Section 7 we found that a generate step is compute-bound once the token batch exceeds the accelerator’s arithmetic intensity $B_\text{crit} = C / W_\text{hbm}$, times $\text{bytes}/2$ for weights not stored in bf16. That calculation assumed every weight we load from HBM gets used by every token. Question 5 of Section 7 notes that an MoE inflates the threshold by $E/k$, so let’s derive it. An MoE layer has to load all $E$ experts (some token in the batch will want each of them), but each token only does FLOPs against $k$ of them:

\[\begin{align*} T_\text{math} &= \frac{2 \cdot B \cdot k \cdot 3DF}{C} \\ T_\text{HBM} &= \frac{E \cdot 3DF \cdot \text{bytes}}{W_\text{hbm}} \end{align*}\]

so we are compute-bound when

\[B > \frac{E}{k} \cdot \frac{\text{bytes}}{2} \cdot \frac{C}{W_\text{hbm}} = \frac{E}{k} \cdot B_\text{crit, dense}\]

Here’s what that gives for a few models with weights in bf16. Halve every number for fp8 weights with bf16 FLOPs, as in Section 8.

Model $E/k$ H800/H100 (296) H200 (206) B200 (281) GB200 (312) TPU7x (311) TPU v5e (240)
DeepSeek-V3, GLM-5, gpt-oss 32 9,500 6,600 9,000 10,000 10,000 7,700
Kimi K2 48 14,200 9,900 13,500 15,000 14,900 11,500
Qwen3.8-2.4T 51 15,100 10,500 14,400 15,900 15,900 12,300
Kimi K3 56 16,600 11,500 15,700 17,500 17,400 13,400

To be compute-bound while decoding DeepSeek-V3, we’d need about ten thousand sequences generating at once in one model replica. At 32k context with DeepSeek-V3’s 35kB-per-token KV cache (Section 14), that’s 11TB of KV cache. So in any realistic deployment, MoE decode is memory-bound on weights, on KV cache, or both, and the arithmetic intensity of the Tensor Core hardly matters. What we can do is spread the $E \cdot 3DF$ bytes of weights over as many GPUs as possible, so that each GPU’s share is small and we’re bound by KV cache bandwidth instead. That’s what DeepSeek does with 144-way expert parallelism at decode, and it’s why Section 14 spends so long on shrinking the KV cache.

What does each expert actually see in that deployment? DeepSeek’s decode step has about 88 sequences on each of 144 H800s (Section 16), so there are 144 * 88 * 8 = 101k token-expert pairs per step spread over 256 routed experts plus 32 redundant copies, or about 101e3 / 288 = 350 tokens per expert copy. Each expert matmul is then a [350, 7168] x [7168, 2048] problem. That’s above the H800’s intensity of 296, so on its own it would be compute-bound. The step as a whole is still nowhere near compute-bound, though, since most of its time goes to loading weights and KV cache from HBM and to the AllToAlls, not to these matmuls (Question 7 comes back to this). The experts only starve at small batch, where we care about a single sequence’s latency: at 8 sequences per GPU we get 8 * 8 * 144 / 288 = 32 rows per expert, half a Tensor Core tile.

Training with FSDP. In Section 12 we found that FSDP on GPUs is compute-bound when the per-GPU batch exceeds $C / W_\text{collective}$, where $W_\text{collective}$ is a GPU’s NVLink egress inside a node or a whole node’s InfiniBand egress across nodes. On an H100 that works out to 2,200 tokens inside a node and 990e12 / 400e9 = 2,475 across nodes, both at bf16 peak. DeepSeek ran fp8 matmuls, which doubles every FSDP threshold in this section (to 4,950 per GPU for a dense model), so we’ll quote both where it matters. Section 5’s TPU version is $C / (M_X W_\text{ici})$, where $M_X$ is the number of ICI axes the gather spans. On v5p with three axes it’s 850 tokens per chip. In every case we compared per-layer FLOPs $\propto B \cdot 3DF$ against per-layer weight-gather bytes $\propto 3DF$. For an MoE the FLOPs are $\propto B k \cdot 3DF$ but the gathered bytes are $\propto E \cdot 3DF$, so

\[\frac{B}{N} > \frac{E}{k} \cdot \frac{C}{W_\text{collective}}\]

For DeepSeek-V3 across H800 nodes that’s 32 * 2475 = 79,200 tokens per GPU at bf16 peak (or 158,400 at the fp8 peak the run actually used). DeepSeek had 62.9e6 / 2048 = 30,700, so they were 2.6x (or 5.2x) short. Kimi K3 would need 56 * 2475 = 139,000. A GB200 NVL72 rack does better. Its 72 GPUs pull each weight shard in once through 72 * 50 = 3,600GB/s of InfiniBand egress and share it over NVLink, so the threshold is 2.5e15 / 3.6e12 = 694 tokens per GPU for a dense model and 32 * 694 = 22,200 for DeepSeek-V3, which is still more than any batch anyone runs. On TPU v5p it’s 32 * 850 = 27,200 tokens per chip, a 244M-token batch on a full pod. So pure FSDP doesn’t work for a fine-grained MoE on any hardware we have. We need another way to shard the experts, and that’s what expert parallelism is.

Takeaway: both the decode batch roofline and the FSDP training roofline are inflated by $E/k$. At sparsity 32 to 64, decode needs ten to twenty thousand concurrent tokens per replica to be compute-bound in the experts. Only the largest expert-parallel deployments reach that, and even there the step is bound by HBM and the network. Pure FSDP needs tens of thousands of tokens per GPU, which isn’t practical. Every MoE deployment we’ll look at shards the experts across GPUs instead.

The Expert-Parallelism Rooflines

Expert parallelism (EP) is what it sounds like: we shard the expert dimension, $W_\text{in}[E_Z, D, F]$ and $W_\text{out}[E_Z, F, D]$, over a mesh axis $Z$, so that each GPU holds $E/Z$ experts. Tokens live on whichever GPU processed the previous layer, so we have to move each token’s activations to the GPUs holding its $k$ experts and move the results back. That’s two AllToAlls per layer (Section 3).

Here’s the full algorithm.

Expert-parallel MoE layer (forward pass):

  1. Scores[BZ, E] = In[BZ, D] *D Wr[D, E] (router; tiny)
  2. Idx[BZ, k], Gate[BZ, k] = TopK(Scores) (decides the AllToAll pattern)
  3. Send[BZ, k, D] = Gather(In, Idx) (k copies of each token)
  4. Recv[(Bk/Z), D] = AllToAllZ(Send) (dispatch: each token goes to the GPUs owning its experts)
  5. Hidden[(Bk/Z), F] = RaggedDot(Recv, Win1[EZ, D, F]) * RaggedDot(Recv, Win2[EZ, D, F]) (grouped by local expert)
  6. Out’[(Bk/Z), D] = RaggedDot(Hidden, Wout[EZ, F, D])
  7. Back[BZ, k, D] = AllToAllZ(Out’) (combine: results return to the token’s home GPU)
  8. Out[BZ, D] = Σk Gate * Back + SharedExpert(In) (weighted sum)

The backward pass is the same thing in reverse: two more AllToAlls, carrying gradients of exactly the same shapes. A shared expert is computed locally (replicated or tensor-sharded like any dense MLP), and since it doesn’t depend on the dispatch, it overlaps with the AllToAlls for free.

Like tensor parallelism, this layout moves activations rather than weights, so its communication is proportional to $B$ and independent of $E$. Unlike tensor parallelism, each token is sent $k$ times, once per expert, so the bytes are $k$ times those of a plain activation reshard.Implementations actually send one copy per destination GPU rather than per expert, so if two of a token's experts share a GPU, only one copy travels. For $Z \gg k$ this is a small correction. DeepSeek-V3's node-limited routing exploits exactly this: confining a token's 8 experts to 4 nodes guarantees at most 4 cross-node copies.

Expert parallelism on GPUs

Let’s compute the roofline in our usual notation, on the machines these models were actually built on. Say we have a global batch of $B$ tokens, EP over $Z$ GPUs, and expert width $F$. Following Section 5 we’ll look at the forward pass, but we’ll count all three expert matmuls rather than two, since for narrow experts the gating matmul is too big to ignore.Section 5 drops the gating matmul and uses $4BDF$ FLOPs per layer to keep the algebra clean. For MoEs with $F = 2048$ we keep all three matrices ($6BDF$). DeepSeek's own overlap analysis in the V4 report also uses the three-matmul count, so the thresholds below match theirs. Be aware of what this does to the numbers: counting all three matrices makes every EP threshold 1.5x more permissive than Section 5's two-matmul convention, and fp8 dispatch makes it another 1.33x more permissive, so under Section 5's $4BDF$, bf16 accounting you would double every $F$ in the tables below. Per GPU we have

\[T_\text{math} = \frac{6 \cdot B \cdot k \cdot D \cdot F}{Z \cdot C}\]

For $T_\text{comms}$, each GPU dispatches $Bk/Z$ token copies of $D$ elements at $b_d$ bytes each, and receives as many back at $b_c$ bytes each. On a switched network (NVLink inside the domain, InfiniBand across it), every byte a GPU sends leaves through its own egress port at $W_\text{egress}$ bytes per second, so as in Section 12

\[T_\text{comms} = \frac{(b_d + b_c) \cdot B \cdot k \cdot D}{Z \cdot W_\text{egress}}\]

Setting $T_\text{math} > T_\text{comms}$, the batch, $k$, $D$ and $Z$ all cancel:

\[F > \frac{(b_d + b_c)}{6} \cdot \frac{C}{W_\text{egress}} = \frac{(b_d + b_c)}{6} \cdot \alpha\]

where $\alpha = C / W_\text{egress}$ is the network operational intensity from Section 12. With bf16 activations both ways ($b_d + b_c = 4$) this is $F > 2\alpha / 3$. With fp8 dispatch and bf16 combine, which is what DeepSeek does, it’s $F > \alpha / 2$. That’s exactly the condition DeepSeek’s V4 report gives for hiding the AllToAll behind expert compute.

This is a surprisingly clean result: whether expert parallelism is compute-bound depends only on the expert width $F$ and on which network the AllToAll crosses. The batch size, $k$, $E$ and the EP degree $Z$ have all dropped out. ($Z$ still matters in one way, since it decides whether we’re still inside the NVLink domain.) Section 12 found the same kind of result for tensor parallelism, where the condition $Y < F W / C$ depends only on $F$ and the network. So instead of asking how much EP we can afford, we can look up $F$ in a table.

This is why people now treat the NVL72 rack, rather than the 8-GPU node, as the basic unit for serving an MoE: a trillion-parameter model’s experts all fit inside one domain where the AllToAll is cheap. So an EP group that spans two racks is worse than a 64-way group that stays inside one, even though it’s larger.

When the expert matmuls can’t hide the AllToAll, the rest of the layer can. Attention in these models is substantial (30% of DeepSeek-V3’s active parameters, plus the quadratic term at long context), and a pipeline schedule that interleaves two micro-batches can run one micro-batch’s attention while the other’s dispatch is in flight. That’s what DeepSeek’s DualPipe schedule does, and it’s also why Kimi K2 kept EP at 16, as we’ll see below. Think of the roofline above as the worst case, where nothing else overlaps. Real training runs land somewhere between it and perfect overlap.

Why not gather instead of dispatch? There’s one alternative layout we should rule out. Instead of dispatching tokens to experts, we could AllGather the tokens across the EP group (as tensor parallelism does), let each GPU run its local experts on whichever gathered tokens routed to them, and ReduceScatter the results. Then

\[T_\text{comms} = \frac{4 \cdot B \cdot D}{W_\text{egress}} \qquad T_\text{math} = \frac{6 B k D F}{Z C}\]

which is compute-bound when $Z < 1.5 \cdot k F / \alpha$. This is just tensor parallelism with an effective MLP width of $kF$. For DeepSeek-V3 ($kF = 16384$) it allows $Z < 11$ over H100 NVLink, so it works inside a node, but only $Z < 1.2$ over InfiniBand, so it never works across nodes. Per local token, dispatch moves $(b_d + b_c) k D = 3kD$ bytes and gathering moves $4ZD$, so dispatch is cheaper whenever $Z > 0.75k$, i.e. $Z > 6$ for $k = 8$. So on a switched network, gathering only wins for EP groups smaller than about $0.75k$ GPUs (6 for $k = 8$), which are too small to matter, and it isn’t used in practice.

Takeaway: on GPUs, expert parallelism with AllToAll dispatch is compute-bound when $F > \alpha / 2$ with fp8 dispatch and bf16 matmuls, or $F > \alpha$ with fp8 matmuls, where $\alpha = C / W_\text{egress}$ for whichever network the tokens cross. The batch, $k$, $E$ and $Z$ don’t enter. Inside an H100 node or a GB200 NVL72 rack, 2,048- to 3,072-wide experts clear it with bf16 matmuls, but with fp8 matmuls only the 3,072-wide experts of V4-Pro and K3 do. Inside an H800 node 2,048 misses even with bf16 matmuls, and across InfiniBand experts miss by 5 to 10x, so the AllToAll has to be overlapped with attention or another micro-batch.

The same rooflines on a TPU torus

On a TPU, the AllToAll no longer leaves through a private port. It has to hop across the torus. From Section 3, an AllToAll that moves $M/N$ bytes per chip on a mesh whose longest axis is $A$ costs $(M/N) \cdot A / (4 W_\text{ici})$ per chip, so

\[T_\text{comms} = \frac{(b_d + b_c) \cdot B \cdot k \cdot D}{Z} \cdot \frac{A}{4 W_\text{ici}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad F > \frac{(b_d + b_c)}{24} \cdot A \cdot \frac{C}{W_\text{ici}}\]

With fp8 dispatch and bf16 combine this is $F > A \cdot \alpha_\text{ici} / 8$. This is the GPU result times a torus factor of $A/4$, where $A$ is the longest axis of the EP sub-mesh and the 4 comes from the bidirectional ring. Here’s the minimum expert width for the TPUs in this book, with fp8 dispatch and bf16 matmuls (double it for fp8 matmuls, as on GPUs):

Chip $\alpha = C / W_\text{ici}$ (bf16) $A = 2$ $A = 4$ $A = 8$ $A = 16$
TPU v5e 2,190 550 1,100 2,200 4,400
TPU v5p 2,550 640 1,280 2,550 5,100
TPU v6e 5,110 1,280 2,560 5,110 10,200
TPU7x 12,800 3,200 6,400 12,800 25,600

These thresholds assume a wraparound link (a bidirectional ring) on the longest EP axis. As Section 2 explains, v5e and v6e only have one on a full axis of 16, and v5p and TPU7x only on axes that are multiples of 4 inside a full cube. So for $A < 16$ on the 2D chips, and for $A = 2$ on any chip, the threshold doubles: a 4x4 block on v6e needs about 5,100 rather than 2,560, and a 2x2x2 on TPU7x needs about 6,400 rather than 3,200.

  • On TPU v5p, a 4x4x4 cube of 64-way EP ($A = 4$) needs $F > 1280$. Every model in our table clears it except Qwen3.5, whose 1,024-wide experts fall 20% short. A 4x4x8 (128-way) needs 2,550, which DeepSeek-V3’s 2,048 doesn’t quite reach.
  • On TPU v6e, a 2D torus, a 4x4 (16-way) EP block has no wraparound and needs $F > 5100$, so DeepSeek-style experts are 2.5x communication-bound and Qwen3.5’s 1,024-wide experts are 5x. Even a 2x2 block ($A = 2$, no wraparound, only 4-way EP) needs 2,560, which DeepSeek’s experts miss by 25%.
  • On TPU7x, a 2x2x2 cube (no wraparound) needs $F > 6400$, and so does a 4x4x4 cube with its wraparound. So by this simple model, none of the fine-grained MoEs in the table are compute-bound under expert parallelism on TPU7x. DeepSeek-V3’s experts fall short by 3x on a cube, and Kimi K3’s by 2x.

A newer chip made the roofline worse! That’s the first time it has happened in this book, so let’s see why. TPU7x has 5x the bf16 FLOPs/s of v5p (2.3e15 against 4.59e14) but exactly the same 9e10 bytes/s per ICI link. Its ICI operational intensity is therefore also 5x higher, 12,800 against 2,550, and every roofline in Section 5 that compares FLOPs to ICI bytes gets tighter by the same factor. Tensor parallelism of a dense model now goes communication-bound beyond $Y > 3 F / 12800$, only about 6-way for LLaMA 3-70B, and FSDP now needs $12800 / 3 = 4270$ tokens per chip instead of 850. Expert parallelism was already the tightest of the three for narrow experts, so on TPU7x the AllToAll can’t hide behind the expert matmuls anymore, and we have to find something else to hide it behind.Ironwood's HBM bandwidth did grow, from 2.8e12 to 7.4e12 bytes/s, so its HBM arithmetic intensity only doubled, from 164 to 311. The design favors per-chip memory bandwidth and fp8 compute, which is to say inference and large-batch training, and its ICI has become the scarce resource for model-parallel training. GPUs went the other way: NVLink bandwidth doubled with each generation's FLOPs, so $\alpha_\text{nvlink}$ stayed between 2,200 and 2,800 from H100 to GB200, and the scarce resource on GPUs is the InfiniBand link out of the NVLink domain.

The gather layout behaves differently on a torus too. Its cost is $4BD / (M_Z W_\text{ici})$, which is compute-bound when $Z < 1.5 \cdot M_Z \cdot k F / \alpha$. For DeepSeek-V3 on v5p with three axes that allows $Z < 29$, and on TPU7x $Z < 6$. Per token, per unit of axis bandwidth, the AllToAll costs $(b_d + b_c) k A / (4 Z)$ and the gather costs $4 / M_Z$. On a 4x4x4 cube with $k = 8$ that’s 3 * 8 * 4 / (4 * 64) = 0.375 against 4 / 3 = 1.33, so dispatching is 3.5x cheaper. On a single ring of 16, though, it’s 3 * 8 * 16 / (4 * 16) = 6 against 4, and gathering wins by 1.5x. So as a rule of thumb, dispatch on a cube and gather on a ring. This rule has no GPU analog, since a switch has no rings.

Takeaway: on TPUs the same roofline picks up the torus factor: expert parallelism is compute-bound when $F > A \cdot \alpha_\text{ici} / 8$ (fp8 dispatch, bf16 matmuls), where $A$ is the longest axis of the EP mesh. On TPU v5p a 64-way EP cube works for 2,048-wide experts. On TPU7x, whose ICI intensity is 5x higher, the same experts are 3x communication-bound, and the AllToAll has to be overlapped with attention or another micro-batch, just as across InfiniBand on GPUs.

Combining expert parallelism with FSDP

In practice EP is one axis of a larger layout. Say we do EP over $Z$ GPUs and data parallelism with sharded weights (FSDP, or ZeRO-3) over $X$, for $N = XZ$ accelerators in total.This $N$ is Section 5's accelerator count. In Section 4, and in Sections 14 and 16, $N$ is the number of attention heads. Each GPU holds $E/Z$ experts and gathers their weights over $X$ every layer, so the FSDP traffic per layer is proportional to $(E/Z) \cdot 3DF$ rather than $E \cdot 3DF$. With $P$ pipeline stages also dividing the weight bytes each GPU gathers (as in Section 12), the FSDP roofline becomes

\[\frac{B}{N} > \frac{E}{k Z P} \cdot \frac{C}{W_\text{collective}}\]

In other words, expert parallelism of degree $Z$ divides the FSDP inflation factor $E/k$ by $Z$. With $Z = E/k$, an MoE has exactly the FSDP roofline of a dense model. That’s $Z = 32$ for DeepSeek-V3, 56 for Kimi K3 and 64 for DeepSeek-V4-Pro. These are the EP sizes used in practice (DeepSeek-V3 trained with EP64), and 64 or 72 is also the largest EP that fits inside one GB200 NVL72 rack, where we just showed the AllToAll is compute-bound. It’s reassuring that the two rooflines land in the same place, since it suggests our simple model is roughly right.

Let’s check this against the one fully published GPU layout. DeepSeek-V3 ran EP64 across 8 H800 nodes, PP16, and 2-way data parallelism with ZeRO-1. (ZeRO-1 shards the optimizer state but replicates the weights, so the per-step cost is a gradient AllReduce rather than a per-layer gather.) Plugging into our formula as if it were FSDP, we need 256 / (8 * 64 * 16) * 2475 = 77 tokens per GPU (155 in fp8), against the 30,700 they had. EP and PP carried almost all of the model parallelism, so data parallelism had almost nothing left to do and 2-way ZeRO-1 was plenty. The hard part of the run was the AllToAll.

On a GB200 NVL72 cluster at fp8 peak, the dense-model FSDP threshold across racks is 5e15 / 3.6e12 = 1,390 tokens per GPU, twice the 694 we found above. With 72-way EP inside each rack and FSDP across racks, DeepSeek-V4-Pro’s threshold is (384 / (6 * 72)) * 1390 = 1,240 tokens per GPU with fp8 matmuls and bf16 weight gathers, or 620 if the gathers are fp8. A 94M-token batch on 9,216 GPUs gives 10,200 per GPU, so there’s plenty of room, and Section 16 works the plan out in full. On a v5p pod the formula reads $B/N > E/(kZ) \cdot C / (M_X W_\text{ici})$, and $Z = 64$ (a 4x4x4 cube, whose AllToAll we showed is compute-bound) gives 32 / 64 * 850 = 425 tokens per chip, against the 7,000 per chip DeepSeek’s batch would give on 8,960 chips, so there’s plenty of room there too.

Takeaway: expert parallelism of degree $Z$ shrinks the MoE FSDP penalty from $E/k$ to $E/(kZ)$, and pipeline parallelism divides it by $P$ again. Setting $Z \approx E/k$ (32 to 64 for current models) gets us back the dense-model roofline, and it’s also about the largest EP that fits inside a GB200 NVL72 rack or stays compute-bound on a TPU v5p cube. With EP and PP in place, the data-parallel axis of a 2026 training run is small and cheap.

DeepSeek-V3 (2,048 H800s, 2024), our main reference, used 64-way EP across 8 nodes, 16-way pipeline parallelism with DualPipe, and 2-way ZeRO-1 data parallelism. Its batch was 15,360 sequences of 4,096 tokens, so 62.9M tokens per step or 30.7k tokens per GPU, with fp8 dispatch, bf16 combine, and each token’s experts limited to 4 nodes. By our roofline the cross-node AllToAll is about 5x communication-bound with bf16 matmuls and 10x with the fp8 matmuls the run used. DualPipe hid part of it behind the other micro-batch’s attention and MLP, with 20 SMs reserved for communication. In Question 7 of Section 4 you computed the achieved utilization as about 22% of fp8 peak, using that section’s H800 figure. With the H800’s actual dense fp8 rate it’s 17%.Section 15 redoes the calculation. DeepSeek's hardware paper reports 385 TFLOP/s per GPU in steady state, which is 19% of fp8 peak once attention FLOPs are counted, or the 39% of bf16 peak they quote. The communication-bound AllToAll is where most of the rest went.

Kimi K2 (H800s, 2025) used 16-way pipeline parallelism with virtual stages, 16-way EP, ZeRO-1 data parallelism, bf16 weights with fp32 gradient accumulation, and fp8 storage (but not compute) for a few insensitive activations. Its cluster used RoCE (RDMA over Converged Ethernet, an Ethernet scale-out fabric) rather than InfiniBand, with 8 x 400Gb/s per node, the same 50GB/s per GPU as in this section’s tables. The report says EP was set to “the smallest feasible” value so that one micro-batch’s attention covers another’s EP AllToAll under a standard one-forward-one-backward (1F1B) pipeline schedule, and that a smaller EP group “relaxes expert-balance constraints”. It also says why Kimi didn’t use DualPipe: that schedule doubles the memory for parameters and gradients, which a 1T-parameter model on 80GB GPUs couldn’t afford. With $Z = 16 < E/k = 48$, the FSDP-equivalent inflation was 3x, which the pipeline stages absorbed.

Kimi K3 (2026) used pipeline parallelism with virtual stages, EP, ZeRO-1, pipeline-aware ZeRO-2 gradient sharding, and context parallelism for the linear-attention layers. Two things are new. Its dispatch library, MoonEP, is the redundant-expert planner described under routing above (the report also proves that at most $E/Z$ redundant expert slots per rank are ever needed). And its shared experts are replicated across EP ranks and run on a separate stream while the AllToAll is in flight, so the AllToAll hides behind a matmul that had to happen anyway.

Gemma 4 26B-A4B (TPU v6e, 2026) is our one TPU MoE data point: 128 experts of width 704 with 8 active, trained on 6,144 TPU v6e chips. Its experts are far narrower than the 5,100 a 4x4 EP block on v6e would need, and its sparsity of 16 is mild, so we’d expect it to have leaned on FSDP over many chips with a small EP factor and a large batch. Unfortunately Google only says that the optimizer state is ZeRO-3 (FSDP-style) sharded under GSPMD, and gives no expert-parallel degree.

None of these runs uses expert parallelism on its own. In every one, the AllToAll hides behind something else: attention, the shared experts, or another micro-batch. The recipe is the same each time, and it’s a good rule of thumb in general: pick an EP degree whose AllToAll your network can hide behind the rest of the layer, then use pipelining and FSDP for whatever parallelism you still need.

What Should You Take Away from this Section?

  • An MoE has $L (E + E_s) 3DF$ parameters and $L (k + E_s) 3DF$ active parameters plus attention, and training costs $6 \cdot \text{active params} \cdot \text{tokens}$ FLOPs. The 2026 frontier open models have total-to-active ratios of roughly 20 to 33 ($E/k$ of 32 to 64) and were trained for 3e24 to 1e25 FLOPs.

  • Every roofline that compares weight bytes to FLOPs is inflated by $E/k$. Decode needs $E/k \cdot B_\text{crit}$ tokens per step to be compute-bound (10k to 20k for current models). Only the largest expert-parallel deployments reach that, and even there the decode step is bound by HBM and the network. Pure FSDP needs $E/k \cdot 2{,}475$ tokens per GPU across H800 nodes (79k for DeepSeek-V3 at bf16 peak and 158k at the fp8 peak it used), 22k on a GB200 rack or 27k on a v5p pod, so no real run does it.

  • Expert parallelism moves activations and costs two AllToAlls per layer. It’s compute-bound when $F > \alpha / 2$ on GPUs with fp8 dispatch and bf16 matmuls ($F > \alpha$ with fp8 matmuls), where $\alpha = C / W_\text{egress}$ of the network the tokens cross, or when $F > A \alpha_\text{ici} / 8$ on TPUs ($A$ = longest EP axis). Inside an H100 node or a GB200 NVL72 rack, 2,048- to 3,072-wide experts clear this with bf16 matmuls, and only the 3,072-wide ones with fp8. Inside an H800 node 2,048 misses even in bf16, and across InfiniBand or on TPU7x experts miss by 3 to 10x, so the AllToAll has to be overlapped with attention or another micro-batch.

  • EP of degree $Z$ cuts the FSDP inflation to $E/(kZ)$, so $Z \approx E/k$ gets us dense-model behavior back, and pipeline parallelism cuts it further. That’s why DeepSeek-V3’s data-parallel axis was only 2 wide.

  • The router is a systems component. It’s kept balanced with per-expert biases instead of dropped tokens, it may be constrained to limit network fan-out, and its inputs may be projected to a latent to shrink what gets sent.

  • The newest hardware looks designed around these models: the GB200 NVL72 rack and the 192GB TPU7x chip both let a trillion-parameter MoE fit in one fast domain. NVLink kept pace with GPU FLOPs, so $\alpha_\text{nvlink}$ stayed near 2,500, and the link to watch on GPUs is the InfiniBand link out of the domain. TPU7x’s ICI didn’t keep pace, so every model-parallel roofline in this book is 5x tighter on it than on v5p.

Question 2 [gpt-oss-120b on one GPU]: Say we want to serve gpt-oss-120b on a single GPU. It has $E = 128$, $k = 4$, $F = D = 2880$ and $L = 36$, and it ships its expert weights in MXFP4 (0.5 bytes per parameter) with everything else in bf16. Its attention alternates 18 full layers with 18 sliding-window layers (128 tokens), with 8 KV heads of dimension 64.

(a) Does it fit on one 80GB H100? How much room is left for KV cache?

(b) What’s the lower bound on decode step time at small batch, and what batch size would we need for the expert matmuls to be compute-bound (bf16 FLOPs)?

(c) At 8k context, how many sequences fit in the remaining memory with a bf16 KV cache, and what are the step time and throughput at that batch?

Click here for the answer.

(a) The expert weights are 36 * 128 * 3 * 2880 * 2880 = 114.7e9 parameters, which at 0.5 bytes is 57.3GB. Everything else (attention, router, embeddings) is about 2.1e9 parameters in bf16, or 4.2GB. That’s 61.5GB in total, leaving about 18.5GB for KV cache and activations. So it fits, which was clearly the design target.

(b) Loading 61.5GB from HBM at 3.35e12 bytes/s takes 18.4ms, so at batch 1 the best we can do is about 54 tokens/s. To be compute-bound in the experts we need $B > (E/k) \cdot (\text{bytes}/2) \cdot C / W_\text{hbm} = 32 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 296 = 2,370$ tokens per step. Four-bit weights cut this by 4x relative to bf16 weights (which would need 9,500), which is a big part of why 4-bit matters so much for MoEs.

(c) Only the 18 full-attention layers hold a cache that grows with context, at 2 * 8 * 64 * 18 * 2 bytes = 36.9kB per token. The sliding layers hold at most 128 tokens each, a fixed 18 * 128 * 2048 bytes = 4.7MB per sequence. At 8k context each sequence costs 8192 * 36.9e3 + 4.7e6 = 307MB, so about 18.5e9 / 307e6 = 60 sequences fit. At batch 60 each step loads 61.5GB of weights and 18.4GB of KV, which takes 24ms, for a throughput of about 2,500 tokens/s on one GPU. That’s nowhere near the 2,370-token batch that would make the experts compute-bound, so the GPU spends the whole step loading weights and KV cache.

Question 3 [expert parallelism for narrow experts]: Say we want to train Qwen3-235B-A22B ($E = 128$, $k = 8$, $F = 1536$, $D = 4096$) on 32 HGX B200 nodes (256 GPUs). (a) What’s the largest EP degree we can use while staying compute-bound? What about with the gather-based layout? (b) What batch size does the FSDP roofline then require? (c) Redo (a) and (b) on a TPU v6e 16x16 slice (256 chips, 2D torus, $\alpha = 5110$). Assume 900GB/s of NVLink per GPU, one 400Gb/s NIC per GPU, and bf16 matmuls with fp8 dispatch.

Click here for the answer.

(a) Inside a node, $\alpha$ = 2.25e15 / 9e11 = 2,500 and the AllToAll needs $F > \alpha / 2 = 1{,}250$. Our 1,536-wide experts clear that, so 8-way EP inside the node works. Across InfiniBand $\alpha = 45{,}000$ and the threshold is 22,500, so the experts can’t leave the node. The gather layout needs $Z < 1.5 k F / \alpha = 1.5 \cdot 8 \cdot 1536 / 2500 = 7.4$, so only 4-way. Dispatch is the better layout here, as everywhere on a switched fabric.

(b) With $Z = 8$ the FSDP inflation is $E / (kZ) = 128 / 64 = 2$. The cross-node FSDP threshold on an 8-GPU B200 node is $C / W_\text{node}$ = 2.25e15 / 4e11 = 5,625 tokens per GPU, so we need 2 * 5625 = 11,250 tokens per GPU, or a global batch of 256 * 11,250 = 2.9M tokens (twice that with fp8 matmuls). That’s a perfectly normal batch for a model this size. The plan is EP8 inside each node and FSDP across the 32 nodes, with the batch doing most of the work.

(c) On v6e, no EP block is compute-bound for 1,536-wide experts. A 2x2 block has no wraparound and needs $F > 2560$, and a 4x4 needs 5,100, both well above 1,536. (The gather layout on a 2x2 without wraparound allows only $Z < 3.6$, so it doesn’t rescue 4-way either.) We could run 4-way EP about 1.7x communication-bound and hide the AllToAll behind attention, or drop EP and use pure FSDP over the 16x16 torus, whose full axes do have wraparound. With inflation $E/k = 16$ that needs 16 * 5110 / 2 = 40,900 tokens per chip, a 10.5M-token global batch on 256 chips, 3.6x the GPU plan’s 2.9M. Either way, Qwen’s 1,536-wide experts are too narrow to spread over a 2D torus of this generation, even though an NVLink node spreads them eight ways with no trouble.

Question 4 [Llama 4’s odd choice]: Llama 4 Maverick uses 128 experts of width 8,192 with $k = 1$ plus one shared expert, in every other layer, and Meta trained it on H100s. Compare it with DeepSeek-V3 on (a) whether expert parallelism across InfiniBand is compute-bound with bf16 dispatch and bf16 matmuls, (b) the decode batch needed to be compute-bound on an H100, and (c) the same two numbers on TPU v5p and TPU v5e. What tradeoff did Meta make?

Answer

(a) With bf16 both ways the GPU condition is $F > 2\alpha / 3$, and across H100 InfiniBand $\alpha = 19{,}800$, so the threshold is 13,200. Maverick’s 8,192-wide experts miss it by 1.6x (1.2x with fp8 dispatch), which is close enough to hide behind attention. DeepSeek-V3’s 2,048-wide experts miss it by 6.4x.

(b) The decode roofline is $E/k \cdot B_\text{crit}$, which is 128 * 296 = 37,900 tokens per step for Maverick against 32 * 296 = 9,500 for DeepSeek-V3 on an H100.

(c) On a v5p cube the condition is $F > A \alpha / 6$, so $A < 6 \cdot 8192 / 2550 = 19$. Any cube up to $A = 16$ works, and Maverick could be expert-parallel across all 128 experts (a 4x4x8 mesh) with margin to spare, while DeepSeek-V3’s experts allow $A < 4.8$, a 4x4x4 cube and no more. On a v5e the decode batches are 30,700 against 7,700.

So what was the tradeoff? Meta picked wide experts that are easy on the network and paid for them with a 4x higher decode batch requirement. DeepSeek made the opposite choice. But at any realistic batch, decode is memory-bound for both models, so the 4x difference in decode batch hardly matters. DeepSeek’s choice costs almost nothing at inference time and buys all the combinatorial benefit of fine-grained routing. That’s probably a big part of why most later models copied DeepSeek rather than Meta.

Question 5 [Kimi K3 on GB200 racks, hard]: Say we want to pretrain Kimi K3 ($E = 896$, $k = 16$, $E_s = 2$, routed expert width $F = 3072$ on a latent of $\ell = 3584$, $D = 7168$, 93 layers of which 92 are MoE) on 128 GB200 NVL72 racks with a 60M-token batch, in fp8 with fp8 dispatch and bf16 weight gathers. Assume 9,216 GPUs at 5e15 fp8 FLOPs/s each, 900GB/s of NVLink egress inside the rack, and one 400Gb/s NIC per GPU, so 3.6TB/s of InfiniBand egress per rack.

(a) Can we use pure FSDP?

(b) Pick an EP degree that makes FSDP compute-bound. Is the EP AllToAll itself compute-bound?

(c) Roughly what fraction of peak could we achieve if the AllToAll weren’t overlapped with anything? Routed experts are about 47% of K3’s active FLOPs (the shared experts, latent projections and attention are the other 53%).

(d) Redo (a) to (c) on a full TPU7x pod of 9,216 chips, with bf16 matmuls and fp8 dispatch, $\alpha = 12800$ and three ICI axes.

Click here for the answer.

(a) The per-GPU batch is 60e6 / 9216 = 6,500 tokens. The FSDP threshold across racks is $C / W_\text{rack}$ = 5e15 / 3.6e12 = 1,390 tokens per GPU for a dense model in fp8, and pure FSDP for K3 needs $E/k$ times that, 56 * 1390 = 77,800. That’s twelve times what we have, so no.

(b) With EP $Z$ inside a rack we need (56 / Z) * 1390 < 6500, so $Z > 12$. Let’s take $Z = 64$ (a rack has 72 GPUs, and 64 divides the 896 experts evenly), which gives a threshold of (56 / 64) * 1390 = 1,220 tokens per GPU, five times below what we have. For the AllToAll inside the rack we need $F > \alpha_\text{fp8} / 2$ = (5e15 / 9e11) / 2 = 2,780 with fp8 matmuls and fp8 dispatch. K3’s experts are 3,072 wide, so the dispatch is compute-bound by 10% with nothing overlapped at all. Both rooflines are satisfied.

(c) Since the AllToAll is compute-bound, our roofline model (which, as everywhere in this book, lets communication overlap with the compute that consumes it) charges it no extra wall-clock time, even with nothing else overlapped. The answer is “whatever the rest of the step allows”: the pipeline bubbles, the attention layers and the HBM traffic of the linear-attention state set the utilization here, not the network.

(d) On TPU7x the per-chip batch is the same 6,500, and pure FSDP needs $E/k \cdot \alpha / 3 = 56 \cdot 4270 = 239{,}000$ tokens per chip, about 37 times what we have. With EP over a 4x4x4 cube ($Z = 64$, $A = 4$) the FSDP threshold drops to (56 / 64) * 4270 = 3,740 tokens per chip, which is fine. But the AllToAll needs $F > A \alpha / 8 = 4 \cdot 12800 / 8 = 6{,}400$, so the dispatch is about 2.1x communication-bound on a cube. (A 2x2x2 has no wraparound and lands at the same 6,400, and $Z = 8$ would leave FSDP needing 29,900 tokens per chip, which we don’t have.) The latent projection doesn’t help the ratio, since it halves the bytes and the FLOPs together. If the routed-expert time is 2.1x its compute time and routed experts are 47% of FLOPs, the step takes 0.53 + 0.47 * 2.1 = 1.5 times its compute-bound duration, so we’d get about 66% of whatever utilization we would otherwise reach. In practice we’d overlap the dispatch with the attention layers and the shared experts (which K3 runs on a separate stream for exactly this reason) and recover most of that. So with the same model, batch and chip count, the AllToAll is free on paper on the GPU cluster and 2.1x over budget on the TPU pod.
